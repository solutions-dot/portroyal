# Air Jamaica: Our Own Wings (`games/air-jamaica/`, not started)

**Status, 24 November 2026: design only.** No code, no bodies, no places. This page holds the idea (owner, 24 November 2026), the chapter plan, the rules and the research still to do. Nothing here has been checked at source yet except the broad outline in "What the first search found". Start from `games/_template/` (`docs/NEW_GAME.md`).

## The idea

The story of Jamaica's own airline, told as national pride and what it cost to keep it flying. It ends with the sale to Caribbean Airlines, the moment the airline stopped being Jamaican. The last question for the player is what Jamaica kept when the tail bird stopped flying.

The owner's themes: national pride, "Jamaica's own carrier", the takeover by Butch Stewart, and the buy-for-a-dollar story (see "The $1 claim").

Audience as everywhere: children 6 and up, plus adults. All-ages, no Mature card. Money, debt and lost jobs are the hard parts, not violence.

## Main character

**Marcia Bennett** (placeholder name, fictional): a young woman from Kingston who joins the airline as a flight attendant on its first year and stays to the end. Her journal page says "Marcia in this game is fictional". Her family on the ground (a mother in Kingston, a brother in England) gives a reason for every route: the plane is how they stay a family.

Real people appear only as the sources allow (see "Real people").

## For children: how much of the sale to tell (owner, 24 November 2026)

The game is for ages 6 and up. The detailed sale terms above stay in this doc and in the journal's "Dig deeper" for older readers. In the story and its dialogue, the sale is one idea in plain words: *the airline owed a lot of money, Jamaica could not keep paying, and the airline from Trinidad and Tobago, Caribbean Airlines, took over the planes and flights. The name and the welcome stayed in people's hearts.* No percentages, dollar amounts or bidder names in dialogue.

**Voice.** Copy the other games (`docs/CONTENT.md`; see `games/shark-papers/src/content.js` and `games/windrush/src/content.js`): lines of about 12 words or fewer, one idea each, `who`/`text` lines, a first-talk list per stage, short `FLAVOUR` lines for later visits, a `JOURNAL` with `simple` and `deeper`, a `SOURCES` table. The player's lines are marked "You".

**A child in the story.** The other kids' games follow a child (Jem, twelve; Iris, nine to seventy-nine). For Air Jamaica the player should be a child too, so a younger player sees themselves in it. Proposal for the owner to confirm: the player is **a child who flies**, with an aunt or mother who is cabin crew, and the story follows the same family across the years (a girl of nine on a first flight in 1969, a young mother in 1994, a grandmother on the last flight in 2010, as Windrush does). Marcia stays as the crew member the child follows. Whichever is chosen, say "fictional" on the journal page.

## Chapters (about six, about 4 to 6 minutes each)

1. **1969: A flag in the sky.** The first flights, April 1969. Marcia's first day: the uniform, the plane, the welcome. Place: Palisadoes airport apron and a cabin.
2. **The pride years.** Families, the diaspora, tourists, rum punch and the Jamaican welcome on board. Place: the cabin on a Kingston to New York flight; a family meets at the gate.
3. **1994: A new owner.** Butch Stewart's group takes the airline. What it meant and what people hoped. Place: an office and the apron with a new look on the tail.
4. **Heavy loads.** The losses, the handback to the government in December 2004, and the question of whether a small country can afford a flag carrier. Place: a crew room, the books.
5. **2010 to 2011: The sale.** Caribbean Airlines takes the routes. Jobs end, some are rehired. Place: the crew room, a hangar, the lounge.
6. **The last Air Jamaica day.** The last Air Jamaica flight and what stays Jamaican: the people, the food, the welcome. End on the ground with Marcia. Place: the apron at dusk, the tail bird.

Every chapter ends the way the other games do: a short journal "Dig deeper" with the sourced facts and what is imagined.

## What the first search found (24 November 2026, not yet checked at source)

Taken from web search summaries only. Read each at source before writing it into the game.

- The airline began flying on **1 April 1969** (incorporated late 1968) from Kingston and Montego Bay to New York and Miami. An earlier Air Jamaica dates to 1963. The partner was **Air Canada**, not Air India (the search summaries say about 40 percent, with Air Canada supplying equipment, training, pilots and some managers; it sold out in 1980, leaving the airline fully state owned). Early fleet: a leased DC-8 and DC-9s, with the 727 later in the 1970s (counts differ).
- **1994:** privatised to the Air Jamaica Acquisition Group led by Gordon "Butch" Stewart. Accounts say the group held about **75 percent** and the government kept **25 percent**; one source said Stewart himself took 30 percent. Settle the figures at source.
- **December 2004:** the government took full ownership again after losses. Flightglobal and the Jamaica Observer both mention Stewart's group putting about **US$20 million** in to cover the change.
- **2010:** the Cabinet approved the sale to Caribbean Airlines. One account dates completion **1 May 2010**, another **2011**. The likely answer: routes and fleet moved in 2010, the shareholding agreement was signed **27 May 2011** (Jamaican Finance Minister Audley Shaw, Trinidad and Tobago Finance Minister Winston Dookeran). Settle the dates at source.
- **Deal:** Jamaica's government took a **16 percent** stake in Caribbean Airlines and kept Air Jamaica's real estate and industrial assets. Sources disagree about the debt: a 2010 Gleaner report says the government took on the debt (US$940 million in liabilities as of February 2010, US$532.6 million of it assumed by the government), while a World Bank summary says Caribbean Airlines took the business without the past liabilities. Do not give a debt number until this is settled.
- **Jobs:** Air Jamaica staff were made redundant from about **12 April 2010**, with redundancy pay reported at US$300 million (check), and Caribbean Airlines rehired some. Counts of staff kept vary between about 900 and 1,000.
- **End:** Air Jamaica ceased all operations in **2015**.

## Cinematic scenes and the little Air Jamaica novelties (owner, 24 November 2026)

The owner wants real cinematic scenes and engaging play, with the small touches people remember about the airline. Each scene uses the shared look-shot (`engine/lookshot.js`, rule 13): black bars, a gliding camera, short story lines one at a time, the autoplayer skips it.

**Scene A: The cockpit (ch 2).** Marcia is called forward. The pilot invites her to the cockpit for the descent into Kingston (or the climb out of Montego Bay). The player sees the instrument panel, the throttles, the windscreen, and the Blue Mountains or the sea coming up, with a close-up of the captain's hands. The captain has few words, all everyday ("Gear down"). A hands-on moment: the player reads out the checklist line by line, or taps the landing lights. Facts to check before building: which aircraft (DC-9, 727 or A300/A310), the real cockpit layout for that type, a real captain only through a source. The Observer has a piece on Jamaica's first female commercial jet pilot, 1979 (name and details not read yet): a good fact for the journal and perhaps the person in the right seat.

**Scene B: The flying chef (ch 3, in the Stewart years).** *Owner, 24 November 2026: the chef is in the story; the year his service began is left out. The story and journal say "in these years", never a start year.* Marcia serves a passenger who is flying for the first time, a boy or an old woman going to see family. The new service has a chef in the cabin who makes the meal in front of them: a trolley, a white jacket, plating each course, champagne breakfast, wine or beer with every meal. The player helps the chef: lay the cloth, choose the next course, carry it down the aisle. The camera eases in on the plate, with sound (a cork, a sizzle), then the words show. What is known so far (summaries, not read at source): after the 1994 privatisation the airline's own history says onboard service was redesigned with "Executive flying chef", fresh Jamaican dishes, champagne breakfasts in first and main cabin, and wine or beer with every meal; a 2004 JIS article calls Louis Bailey "the only flying chef in the industry". The dishes now have a source: the 2004 launch menu was ackee and saltfish, calaloo and saltfish, run dung, festival and jerk chicken (JIS, 21 March 2004; see "Read at source"). Use only those, or another dish from a real menu card. Never invent a dish and call it the real one. No year is given for when the flying chef began, in the scene, the dialogue or the journal. If the chef is named, use the real name only as the sources give it (Louis Bailey, called "the only flying chef in the industry" in 2004), and say his words in the game are imagined.

## Read at source, 24 November 2026

Pages read in full (text pulled with `curl`). Where a number differs between sources, both are given.

- **1963 and 1969.** AirlineGeeks (2016): the first Air Jamaica was founded 27 August 1963, owned 51% by the government, 33% BOAC and 16% BWIA. The second, from 1968, was 60% government and 40% **Air Canada**, which also gave aircraft, pilots, maintenance and help. It began flying **1 April 1969**, Kingston and Montego Bay to New York and Miami, with one DC-8 and three DC-9s. The 727 came in the late 1970s. FundingUniverse puts Air Canada's sale of its share in **1980**. Added routes: Toronto, Montreal, San Juan, Philadelphia, London from **1974**.
- **1994.** FundingUniverse: in May 1994 a group of Jamaican and Canadian investors agreed to buy 70% for **US$26.5 million**; the government kept 25%. Ownership passed in **November 1994** and Trevor Boud became CEO. Stewart was chairman. New logos and livery came in; the airline gave free **champagne, Red Stripe beer and rum punch**. Within a year it was profitable.
- **The Observer, 23 December 2022** (18 years to the day after the handback): on **23 December 2004** Stewart "handed back Air Jamaica to the Government, gave it US$20 million to fund the transition". The paper says he introduced "on-time, no-line schedules, champagne flights and the flying chef", bought new planes and had a bond with the market traders, the "ICIs" (mostly women, once called higglers), who loaded the planes with boxes other airlines turned away. It calls the airline "the little piece of Jamaica that flies". Stewart is quoted as saying "Partnering Air Jamaica with the Government was a nightmare" and that a 1999 debt-swap agreement was never signed. That is his side; the journal must say so. The same article tells of the late 1980s, when drug smuggling on the planes brought US fines (a US$35 million fine cut to under US$3 million), before Stewart. Keep that out of the main story; at most one gentle journal line.
- **The flying chef, JIS, 21 March 2004.** "The only flying Chef in the industry", **Louis Bailey**, who flew on several flights a week, talked to passengers and asked for feedback on the dishes. Dishes served at the Toronto launch of the return flights (5 April 2004, after 14 years away): **ackee and saltfish, calaloo and saltfish, run dung (rundown), festival and jerk chicken**. This is the real menu for scene B. The Observer lists the flying chef among Stewart's changes. The game gives no start year (owner, 24 November 2026).
- **The first woman pilot.** Observer, 20 March 2010: **Maria Ziadie (Ziadie-Haddad)** joined Air Jamaica in **1979** as a Boeing 727 second officer, one of the first women airline pilots in the Western hemisphere. First all-female flight on an A320, December 1997, JM 039 Kingston to Fort Lauderdale (reported as the first such in the Western hemisphere). She became a 727 captain in July 1996. Good for the cockpit scene (see below), only through her words in the sources, or with the journal saying her words are imagined.
- **The sale to Caribbean Airlines.** JIS: Cabinet approved the divestment effective **Friday 30 April 2010** (first due 12 April, extended by agreement); the government was "spending some US$200 million on meeting the airline's outstanding obligations during the transitional period". Gleaner, 5 March 2010: staff to be laid off around 12 April; Caribbean Airlines would fly on Air Jamaica's existing certificate, fleet, **logo and colours** while it built its own brand; staff were put through tests to decide who was re-employed; Trinidad had already spent more than TT$500 million on Caribbean Airlines, and some in Trinidad opposed the deal. Gleaner, 3 May 2011: 54 Air Jamaica staff were not kept when their contracts ended on 30 April 2011, the rest were on short contracts; one worker said officials told them "there is no Air Jamaica; the name is only a marketing tool". AirlineGeeks: Caribbean Airlines took the routes and fleet on **1 May 2010**, and Air Jamaica stickers stayed under the new logo until **2015**. A YouTube clip is titled "The Last Air Jamaica Departure JM017 on 4/30/10 at 10:34pm" (the title only: not yet viewed). Still to find: the 16 percent stake, the debt split (the World Bank PDF text did not search cleanly), and who the last pilot and crew were.

- **1969 crew and uniforms (Wilson Center blog by Phil Tiemeyer, Kansas State University, citing the Daily Gleaner of 9 February and 1 April 1969).** The Gleaner ran an eight-page special on 1 April 1969. The airline had **58 stewardesses**, headlined "Some rare tropical 'birds'". The planes were "painted a bold mix of colors", and the dresses were short-sleeved minis, "a five inch above the knee mini", in "green, lime yellow, and cerise", each trimmed with one of the other colours. They were designed by **Marguerite Kirkpatrick**, a former Miss Jamaica who ran a fashion and modelling company and became the first head of In-Flight Services, and ran the stewardess training. On long flights the stewardesses modelled outfits and swimwear from Jamaican designers to island music (the "fashion shows"). The Doctor Bird logo was called the "Love Bird", for the airline and for the stewardesses. Over 80 percent of passengers were American tourists. The author's view is that the "birds" marketing was copied from Braniff and the US, and was sexist. A journal page for children has to say this plainly and gently: the fashion shows were real and loved, and the way the women were sold was unfair. The game shows the clothes and the fashion show, never the "strip" idea.
- **A different start date.** The Gleaner of 1 May 2010 ("Air Jamaica celebrates for the last time") counts the airline's 44th birthday on 1 May 2010, so it dates the start to **1966**. It describes flights from 2 May 1966 under BOAC and BWIA, an all-Jamaican crew of three captains (Herbert Steele, Michael Guilfoyle, John Purchas), a Sunjet carrying 102 passengers, and "gleaming blue and white plane steps marked 'Air Jamaica'". It says the airline made a profit in its first 18 months, then linked with Air Canada, and that fashion shows, Jamaican dishes with rum and Red Stripe were features from the early flights. So 1963 (company), 1966 (first flights) and 1969 (the Air Canada airline) are all real dates of different steps. The game's first chapter uses **1 April 1969** (the launch of the airline the player knows) and says the earlier start in the journal.
- **SkyWritings is older than Stewart.** The Wilson Center blog cites an issue of the in-flight magazine *Sky Writings*, September 1972. An earlier note in this doc, from a summary, said Stewart started it. That is wrong. Do not say so.
- **The sale terms, World Bank/IFC brief (February 2011).** Signed **30 April 2010**; effective **1 May 2010**. Trinidad and Tobago would put in **US$50 million** of equity; Jamaica got a **16 percent** minority interest in Caribbean Airlines "in compensation for the transfer of Air Jamaica's market share and goodwill" and kept Air Jamaica's real estate and industrial assets. Caribbean Airlines "assumed full financial responsibility" for Air Jamaica and did not take past liabilities. Air Jamaica employees were made redundant, with about **1,000** to be re-hired during the transition. Air Jamaica had lost money in **40 of its 42 years**, with an accumulated deficit of about **US$1.54 billion** (March 2010). The first bidder chosen was Indigo Partners; after four months of talks that ended by mutual consent, and the government went back to Caribbean Airlines. About 900,000 passengers a year were expected. Not in the brief: how much of the old debt the Jamaican government carried (JIS says it was spending about US$200 million on the airline's obligations in the transition). Treat the debt as: Caribbean Airlines did not take it, the state did. Settle the figure before it is shown.
- **Louis Bailey** (Gleaner, 28 January 2010): later an executive chef at a Montego Bay seafood restaurant, "who earned the reputation 'Air Jamaica's Flying Chef'". The start year of the flying chef service is not known and is **not needed**: the game leaves it out (owner, 24 November 2026).

- **Planes and colours.** AirlineReporter (Dec 2012) and departedflights2.com: the fleet over the years included the DC-8, DC-9, Boeing 727, a leased 747-100, Airbus A300, A320, A321 and A340, and later Boeing 737-800s (under Caribbean Airlines). The livery used from 1969 to 1995 is shown on an A300, 6Y-JMK, at Miami on 10 March 1993; an updated livery shows on the same plane in February 1996. A World Airline News photo caption says the A320 6Y-JMF wore "the 2006 livery, the last color scheme worn by the JM fleet". Colours of the 1969 jets are only described as a bold mix. Photos of each livery still have to be looked at by a person.
- **The last Air Jamaica flight.** World Airline News (3 May 2010, citing the Observer): the last "Lovebird" flight was **JM 036, Fort Lauderdale to Kingston, an Airbus A320, on 30 April 2010**, and Caribbean Airlines took over on 1 May. A YouTube clip is titled as the last departure "JM017" that night. The two are different flights, so the game can say "the last Air Jamaica flight home" and does not need to name a number.
- **What people remember** (Jamaicans.com, 2017 and 2026; opinion pieces, not records): the bold, bright uniforms; a warm greeting and smile; asking nicely for "a likkle brawta" and getting extra food; crew who answered questions about customs and where to get jerk chicken; "ackee and saltfish breakfasts", "rum punch on boarding", reggae, aerobics on long flights, and passengers applauding a safe landing. Kaye Chong, a former executive, says the airline "was an extension of Jamaica itself" and that it supported jobs such as pilots, engineers, caterers and upholsterers. Use these as colour in the cabin, not as dated facts.

- **The 1994 colours and launch (Observer, "Air Jamaica: end of an era", 7 and 16 July 2011, read in full).** A photo caption shows "the new-look Air Jamaica aircraft on the tarmac at the Norman Manley International Airport before its inaugural flight in **December 1994**", and another shows Stewart "addressing guests at the launch of the new Air Jamaica in December 1994". So the new look flew in December 1994 (the Gleaner said the first aircraft in the new colours would leave the hangar on 22 November). An earlier search summary said Prime Minister P J Patterson was at the ribbon-cutting. The article does not say that; **do not show him there**. Who designed the new look or the tail is still not found. The same article: Stewart "quickly re-imaged the airline" and brought in "on-time no-line schedules, champagne flights and the flying chef"; the fleet grew from nine ageing aircraft to **20 Airbus aircraft**; at its peak the airline carried **69 percent of all people travelling to Jamaica and 52 percent of tourists**; Stewart said the "flying chef" and "champagne flights" made other airlines debate copying them; and he said "we were like the little mouse that roared louder than the lion." **Careful:** Stewart chaired the Observer's owner, and the article gives his side (it also says his group lost about US$674 million in ten years, and the state lost more after 2004). The journal must say whose view each claim is. For children: it was loved, it won awards, and it lost a lot of money.

## The $1 claim

The owner remembers "buy for $1 or sell for $1". One site (ticker.com) says the airline was handed back to the government for J$1 in 2004. Every other source found, including the Observer piece read in full on 24 November 2026, describes money going the other way (US$20 million from Stewart's group). **Do not put "$1" in the game** unless a first-hand source (a Gleaner or Observer report of the time, a Hansard, or the sale papers) is read and says so. If it checks out, it becomes a good journal fact: a symbolic price for a very large debt.

## Rules for this game

- All-ages. No violence. The hard parts are loss and debt, told gently and plainly.
- **Plain words for money.** Say what a debt is in one short line. Big numbers go in the journal, not in dialogue.
- **No blame.** The sale is a trade-off: people wanted a national airline, the country carried the cost. Show both sides, say neither was wrong.
- **How it looks** (CLAUDE.md rule 3): each aircraft type and livery, uniform, terminal and lounge is modelled on photographs or plans of that very thing, with the years. The Air Jamaica colours and the tail bird change over time, so each chapter needs its own livery and uniform. If a stand-in is unavoidable, say so here and list it as work to do. Logos are shown only as the real ones in the sources.
- **Look and do, close-up** (rules 13 and 14). Candidate tasks, each a real thing to see or do:
  - Walk a family to their gate and check the boarding pass (ch 1 to 2).
  - Look over the tail bird up close as the new colours are painted (ch 3).
