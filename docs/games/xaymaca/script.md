## Journal pages

### You and Maima

- You live in Maima, a Taino village on a hill.
- Below it is the bay the Spanish called Santa Gloria.
- Today it is St Ann's Bay, Jamaica.

Dig deeper:

- You, Grandmother and Cousin are fictional. Your thoughts and your family's talk are imagined.
- Every fact about 1503 and 1504 comes from Spanish writers: Ferdinand Columbus, Diego Mendez and Columbus himself. No Taino account was written down.
- Archaeologists found Maima on the hill above the bay, about a quarter of a league (1.4 km) from where the ships were beached.

### Cassava bread

- Cassava bread was the main food of the Taino.
- It was baked in flat cakes on a clay griddle.
- Jamaicans still make it. Today it is called bammy.

Dig deeper:

- The griddle is called a buren. At Maima, pieces of burens were found by the houses.
- The juice of bitter cassava is poisonous, so it was squeezed out before baking.
- Ferdinand Columbus calls the bread 'zabi'. The village of Maima fed the Spanish with it for a year.

### Cousin

- Cousin is seventeen, and the best paddler in the village.
- Taino people made canoes from one tree trunk.

Dig deeper:

- Cousin in this game is fictional.
- The words canoe and hammock came into English from Taino languages.

### The two ships

- Columbus came to Jamaica in June 1503 with two ships.
- Shipworms had eaten holes in them. They were sinking.
- He ran them onto the sand, side by side.

Dig deeper:

- The ships were La Capitana and the Santiago, called the Bermuda.
- The sailors put straw roofs on the decks and lived there.
- Columbus kept his men on the ships, so they would not take food or quarrel with the villages.
- The ships stood about a bowshot from the shore. The game draws them nearer, so you can see them.

### Trading

- The Taino paddled out to the ships to trade.
- Two hutias for a piece of tin.
- Cassava bread for two or three glass beads.

Dig deeper:

- A big load of food earned a hawk's bell.
- A cacique might get a red cap, a small mirror or scissors.
- Ferdinand Columbus, the Admiral's son, wrote these prices down. He was about fifteen.

### The hawk's bell

- A small round bell, made for a hunting bird's leg.
- The Taino had never heard a sound like it.

Dig deeper:

- The Spanish traded hawk's bells all over the Caribbean.
- Some have been found in Taino sites by archaeologists.

### The interpreter

- A Taino man from Hispaniola sailed with the ships.
- He spoke for the Spanish.

Dig deeper:

- He was real, but no record gives his name.
- His words in this game are imagined, except where he passes on what the Admiral said. That part follows Ferdinand Columbus's account.
- His shirt is imagined too.

### Diego Mendez

- Diego Mendez was real. He was Columbus's secretary.
- He offered to cross the sea in a canoe to get help.

Dig deeper:

- His words in this game are imagined. In real life he spoke Spanish, and the interpreter passed his words on.
- Mendez wrote about the journey years later, in his will.
- He traded a brass helmet, a coat and a shirt for a good canoe.
- He and a cacique called Ameyro swapped names, as a sign of friendship.

### The paddlers

- In July 1503 two canoes set off for Hispaniola.
- Each had six Spaniards and ten Taino paddlers.
- No paddler's name was written down.

Dig deeper:

- Bartholomew Fieschi led the second canoe. Columbus's brother saw them off from the east end of Jamaica.
- Mendez's own account is different. He says he tried twice, the first time with six Taino.
- The record does not say that any paddler came home. We do not know what happened to them.

### The crossing

- It was very hot, and the water ran out.
- One paddler died of thirst.
- Then the moon rose behind a little island, Navassa.

Dig deeper:

- On Navassa they found rainwater in holes in the rock.
- Some paddlers drank too much and died. Others were sick.
- The next day they reached Hispaniola. Nobody would paddle back to Jamaica.
- This is what Mendez told Ferdinand Columbus, who wrote it down.

### The ten canoes

- On 2 January 1504, 48 Spaniards turned against Columbus.
- They took ten canoes and went east.
- They tried to cross the sea, but the waves turned them back.

Dig deeper:

- Their leaders were two brothers named Porras.
- At sea they were cruel to their Taino paddlers. Eighteen paddlers died.
- Then they walked from village to village, taking food by force.

### The cacique

- A cacique was a leader of a village or of many villages.
- The word cacique comes from the Taino.

Dig deeper:

- Maima had a cacique, but no record gives his name. His words here are imagined.
- Ferdinand Columbus wrote that one Spaniard ate as much in a day as a Taino ate in twenty.

### The red moon

- On 29 February 1504 the moon went dark and red.
- Columbus knew it would happen. He read it in a book of tables.
- He said it was his God's anger.

Dig deeper:

- The book was an almanac: it showed when eclipses would happen.
- The moon rose already darkened. People came running with food.
- Ferdinand Columbus wrote that the Taino had seen eclipses before. What frightened them was a man who knew it beforehand.
- NASA's tables say this eclipse was total, and that the moon was dark for about 48 minutes.

### The fight on the hill

- In May 1504 the Spaniards who had left came back.
- They fought Columbus's brother's men near Maima.

Dig deeper:

- Ferdinand Columbus wrote that the Taino stood armed and watched, and took neither side.
- Some Spaniards were killed. The rest asked to be forgiven.

### The ship from Hispaniola

- Diego Mendez bought a ship on Hispaniola and sent it.
- On 28 June 1504 everyone sailed away.

Dig deeper:

- They had been in Jamaica for a little more than a year.
- The old ships were left on the sand of St Ann's Bay.

### Seville Heritage Park

- Today the hill is Seville Heritage Park, in St Ann.
- It has the remains of Maima, a Spanish town and a later estate.

Dig deeper:

- Miss Sharon, the guide, is fictional.
- The Jamaica National Heritage Trust looks after the park.
- Archaeologists dug at Maima in 2014 and 2015.

### A Taino house

- The house that was dug up was round, about 4 metres across.
- One pole held up the middle of the roof.
- A family lived in it.

Dig deeper:

- The houses stood on flat steps cut into the hill.
- The door faced west, away from the wind.
- The cooking was done outside, away from the house.

### Words from the Taino

- Canoe, hammock, hurricane and barbecue come from Taino words.
- So does cacique.
- Xaymaca was the Taino name for Jamaica.

Dig deeper:

- Xaymaca is often said to mean land of wood and water.
- The Taino had important beliefs and ceremonies. This game does not show them, out of respect.

### What people wore

- The weather was warm, and people wore very little.
- Married women wore a short cotton skirt.

Dig deeper:

- In this game every Taino person wears a little more cotton than the old records describe. This was chosen for a children's game.
- People also painted their skin, red and black, on special days.
